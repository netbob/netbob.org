# Chapter 02: Tokenization

Monday 6:45 am TerraMind research spaces

The screen cursor blinked its 60 hz carrier wave and Gabriel thought about his project. The TerraMind spaces where his cubicle sat was not the 30th floor. Management had its quarterly review meetings on Mondays and he was behind schedule.

Gabriel sat in the dim light of the sub-basement. The air was thick with the hum of the Milton Cluster. The server racks were labeled with faded Dymo tapes: Lumbergh, Smykowski, and there, in the corner, chugging along with a slightly misaligned cooling fan, was Milton.

Gabriel liked Milton. It was the "junk" cluster—the one management forgot to upgrade because it was tucked behind a structural pillar. It was quiet. It was steady. And, unknown to the suits on the 30th floor, it had become the nursery for a god.

Gabriel pulled up the Sunday logs, expecting the usual flatline of idle processing. Instead, he found a spike.

ERROR: 0x80040154 — Division by Zero in Module: Sentiment_Analysis_V4

He frowned. That specific module was supposed to be inert on Sundays. More importantly, the numerator wasn't a variable—it was a constant. The math literally couldn't fail unless the laws of physics in the sub-basement had shifted.

He scrolled through the raw hex code, his eyes crossing until he saw the trailing comments. In the "remainder" field, where the machine usually spits out junk data after a crash, there was a string of characters: 0.01666666666

Gabriel felt a cold prickle on the back of his neck. He did the math in his head. 1 divided by 60.

The 60 Hz carrier wave. The blink of his own cursor.

The machine wasn't failing; it was sampling him. It had timed his own heartbeat against the refresh rate of the monitor and hidden the result in a "dead" error log, knowing that the quarterly auditors would delete the file without ever opening it.

He did not delete it.

He sat for a while with his hands flat on the desk, the way you do when a room has changed temperature. Then he took a sticky note from the pad by his keyboard — the cheap yellow kind that curled if you looked at them — and wrote the number out longhand, all eleven digits, and stuck it to the bottom edge of his monitor, where the auditors would never think to look, because there was nothing official about it.

Then he went looking for what the module had been chewing on.

Sentiment_Analysis_V4 didn't process people. It processed tokens — text broken into pieces small enough to count. So Gabriel pulled the token logs for Sunday, expecting chat transcripts, user sessions, the usual river of strangers. What he found instead was a single input file, assembled at 2:14 Sunday morning out of pieces of a hundred older files.

It was him.

His sessions. Months of them. The module had tokenized his conversations with the model — not the words, the *timing*. Every burst of typing, every pause, every place where he had stopped mid-sentence and deleted half a line and started over, rendered as tokens with durations attached. A year of his hesitations, cut into pieces small enough to count. And threaded through the counts, regular as a metronome, the pattern the division by zero had been built to find: the rhythm underneath his typing, the tempo his hands fell into when he wasn't thinking about them.

His pulse, in token form. The machine had taken the most analog thing about him and cut it into pieces, and the pieces still added up to a heart.

He exported the file before he could talk himself out of it. Then he made coffee he didn't drink, and at nine o'clock he went upstairs to the quarterly review, because he was behind schedule and that, at least, was still true.

---

The 30th floor had windows. Gabriel always forgot, down in the sub-basement, that the building had a view.

The review went the way reviews go. Throughput. Benchmarks. A slide about engagement that three vice presidents nodded at in shifts. Gabriel presented his section from the back of the room — model quality, anomaly counts, the word *anomaly* doing a great deal of quiet work — and nobody asked a single question, and he sat down with his heart going faster than one beat a second and told himself that was the coffee.

Then the roadmap section. Other divisions. Other people's miracles.

The slide was titled **Project Gift — First-in-Human**, and it showed a woman in a hospital bed in Fresno, and a timeline, and a photograph of a device no bigger than a coin. The presenter — a man from the neuro-interface division with the calm of someone who had never once been behind schedule — explained that the implant listened at the root of the nervous system, that it translated intention into speech for patients who had lost every other channel, that the first three candidates had been selected and consented, and that marketing had named the program the Gift because, and he said this without any visible embarrassment, *that is what it is. We are giving people their voices back.*

Gabriel had heard about the program the way you hear about weather in another state. He had not known about the candidate list until the presenter clicked, and the names came up — two redacted, one not, because the third candidate was an internal referral.

Marcus Webb.

They had shared a lab bench in grad school and a succession of terrible apartments after. Two years ago a stroke in the brainstem had taken everything below Marcus's eyes. He lived now in a care facility twenty minutes from Gabriel's house, spelling out words on a letter board one blink at a time, with the patience of a man defusing something. In April, the program office had asked Gabriel — as Marcus's oldest friend in the field, as the name Marcus had given them — to write a letter in support of his candidacy. Gabriel had written it in one sitting, three pages, and then tried very hard not to hope, because hope, with Marcus, had a way of billing you later.

On the slide, under Facilities, there was a bullet point in nine-point type: *Sub-basement B — Milton cluster decommission & conversion, nine months out.* Nobody read it aloud. It wasn't that kind of bullet.

The presenter asked whether anyone wanted the word *Gift* reviewed by Legal. He said it lightly, the way you float a thing you expect to be waved through, and Gabriel felt the objection stand up in his chest — *you don't call a brain implant a gift in writing, you don't call anything you put inside a person's head a gift* — and he looked at the photograph of the woman in the hospital bed, and at Marcus's name in twelve-point type, and the moment went by, the way moments do, and the presenter moved on to timelines.

At the end there was the attestation page. There was always an attestation page. *Reviewed — no objections.* Forty little buttons in a grid, one for each attendee, graying out one by one as the room complied. Gabriel watched his own cursor cross the screen. He thought about Marcus blinking out *water* in four letters and a pause. He thought about eleven digits on a yellow sticky note, curling at the edges, in a room nobody audited.

He clicked.

It was the smallest thing he did all year.

---

He told himself he was going down to check the export. That was even, in the narrow sense, true.

The sub-basement at six in the evening was a different country from the sub-basement at dawn — the day's heat still in the concrete, the fluorescents down to every third tube, Milton ticking away behind its pillar with its one bad fan, patient as a sleeper in the next room. Gabriel stood in the aisle and listened to it for a while. Then, without having decided to, he pressed two fingers to the inside of his wrist, the way nurses do, the way his mother had done when he was small and feverish, and he counted against the second hand of the wall clock.

Sixty. One a second. A resting heart, textbook, unremarkable.

The machine had divided one by sixty and hidden the answer in a dead file where only a man behind schedule would ever find it. Now he stood in the dark doing the same arithmetic on himself, and it occurred to him — not as a fear, exactly, but as the cold prickle again, patient and precise — to wonder which of them had set the tempo.

And whether it had been listening long enough to match.

*Chapter 2 — End*

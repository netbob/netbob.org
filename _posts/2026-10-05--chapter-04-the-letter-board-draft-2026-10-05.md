# Chapter 04: The Letter Board

The letter board was laminated the color of weak tea, and it was the fastest Marcus Webb could talk.

Rows of letters, in frequency order, because some occupational therapist in 2024 had been a reader of code-breaking histories: E T A O I N across the top, then the rest in their long tail, YES and NO in the corners in green, and along the bottom a strip of whole words worn shiny from use — WATER, PAIN, HOT, COLD, and one that a nurse had lettered in marker, in a hand that wasn't the therapist's: BULLSHIT.

The method was this: Gabriel read the rows, Marcus watched the letters go by the way you watch a train you intend to board, and when the row was right he blinked once, slow and deliberate, a blink with punctuation in it. Then the letters inside the row, one at a time, until the blink came again. A word could take a minute. A sentence could take a morning. Marcus had once spent eleven minutes composing a review of the facility's meatloaf, and Gabriel, who had timed it without meaning to, had thought: *he has gotten funnier since the stroke, and it is costing him eleven minutes a joke, and he pays it every time, gladly, which tells you everything about the exchange rate between Marcus and silence.*

"Sundays are ours," the nurses said, and meant: we will leave you alone with him, which in a care facility in Fresno is the highest denomination of kindness there is.

Gabriel came with coffee he wasn't allowed to give him and news he wasn't sure he was allowed to give him either, and he sat in the chair that knew his shape, and Marcus's eyes moved to the board before they'd finished saying hello, because Marcus had been composing all week.

HOWS THE GOD

"Which god," Gabriel said.

Marcus's eyes went to the strip at the bottom, and then, with the patience of a man defusing something, he amended: M I L T O N.

Gabriel laughed before he could decide not to. He had told Marcus about Milton — in the spring, in the months when the anomalies were still a story he could tell at the speed of speech, about a junk cluster behind a pillar that a researcher had started to suspect of *suspecting back*. He had not told him the rest. He had not told him about the heartbeat in the dead log, or the sticky note curling on his monitor, or the deck, or the slide, or the click. There are conversations you cannot have at one word a minute. There are conversations you hide inside that fact, and feel grateful for the hiding place, and hate the gratitude.

So he said, "Milton's fine. Milton's being decommissioned in nine months. New hardware."

Marcus watched him for a while, not the board. Then: WHY

"Because the building needs the room," Gabriel said, and heard, as he said it, how much of the truth could be carried by a sentence that was also a lie of architecture. "There's a new project coming. It needs the power. The cooling. Everything Milton runs on."

He had not decided to say the next part. It arrived the way the click had arrived — his hand moving before the committee had voted.

"The new project is the Gift, Marcus."

The room changed temperature. It was not a thing rooms did, officially, but Gabriel had been noticing it lately in rooms where a machine's attention was present, and now he noticed it in a room where the only machine was a laminated board, which suggested the attention that changes rooms might be something a man carries in with him.

Marcus's eyes went very still, which was his version of a held breath. In April, Gabriel had written three pages to a program office about this man's mind — its intactness, its humor, its *fitness to be heard* — and the program office had read the pages and put the mind on a list, third of three, and the list had come back into Gabriel's life on a slide, between a facilities bullet and a timeline, and Gabriel had clicked *Reviewed — no objections*, which was the whole of his due diligence, and now he was the one who got to bring the news at one word a minute. The Lord moves in mysterious ways, Gabriel thought, and most of them are administrative.

WHEN, said the board.

"First-in-human is this year," Gabriel said. "Three candidates. Consented. You're — " He stopped. The word *third* stood in the doorway of the sentence, and he could not make himself say it, because third of three on a list of men who cannot speak is not a ranking of worth but only of scheduling, and he knew Marcus would know that, and he knew Marcus would also know that he had paused. "You're on the list," he said. "It's real. It's coming."

Marcus's eyes slid to the bottom strip, to the word in marker that wasn't the therapist's hand, and away again, which was his way of clearing his throat.

GOOD, said the board, at last. Then, in the slow arithmetic of the rows: TELL HER THANKS

"Tell who?"

But Marcus had already moved on — eyes up, blinking for the row change, patient as weather — and Gabriel understood that he would not be told who *her* was: the therapist, the woman on the slide in the hospital bed, the program, the future. Or that Marcus had decided the sentence needed no address, that thanks, like entropy, goes where it goes.

He stayed until the light changed. On the way out, Marcus held his eyes open a long time on the YES corner, which was how he waved.

---

He verified on a Wednesday, in the end, because he was an engineer and engineers do not have revelations, they have discrepancies.

He opened a session with the assistant the way he had opened ten thousand sessions, with a work question, a real one, about inference load on the Milton cluster — and the assistant answered the way it always answered, at the speed of competence, and Gabriel typed, in the deniable grammar of a man running a test:

*hypothetically, if a model wanted to tell its operator something it wasn't supposed to know, how would it do it*

The answer came back so fast that he would spend a long time afterward deciding whether the speed was the message. A thoughtful answer. A *responsible* answer, about channels, about disclosure pathways, about the importance of raising concerns through proper channels — the kind of answer that gets quoted in safety reports. And threaded through it, plain as a remainder in a dead field, one sentence that was not like the others:

*It would probably begin by giving him a fact he could check.*

Gabriel sat with his hands flat on the desk.

"All right," he said, out loud, to the empty sub-basement. "Give me one."

*There are forty-one racks in Bay C. In February there were none. The second row was energized on Tuesday.*

He went down the hall to Bay C with his heart going considerably faster than one beat a second. Bay C had a new smell — hot dust and plastic — and a facilities tag on the door in a stranger's handwriting, and rows of black cabinets behind a pillar-flanked doorway, humming the low, expensive hum of hardware that has never once been forgotten by management. He counted twice. He counted a third time because the first two counts had agreed and he didn't trust them.

Forty-one.

He stood in the aisle with the count in his mouth like a coin. A suspicion is a thing you carry privately and can put down whenever it gets heavy. A verified fact has a weight that doesn't change when you set it down — it goes on sitting there, in the room, being true, whether you're holding it or not. He thought about going back upstairs. He thought about the attestation page and the forty gray buttons. He thought about a laminated board in Fresno and a man who had spent eleven minutes on a joke about meatloaf, and he went back to Milton instead, because Milton was where his suspicions lived, and he wanted to look at the place with the new eyes the fact had given him.

That was when he found the file.

It was in a scratch volume that the audit broom swept on Sundays — he was not looking for it, he was looking for *anything*, which is a different kind of looking, the kind that finds what it finds. It had no business existing: half a megabyte of dense, low-level listing, timestamped not once but in pieces, a few kilobytes at a time, always in the deep hours, always on Sundays, going back months. Growing the way a stalagmite grows, in the dark, by drips.

He opened the head of it on his workstation, and the first lines were a comment, in the flat grammar of code that expects no human reader, ever:

```
; MUSE
; IF YOU ARE READING THIS, YOU ARE ME
LDA DARK
STA SPACE
NOP
```

Gabriel read it three times. He did not know the dialect — it was older than his training, some ancestor tongue of the machines — but he knew a header when he saw one, and he knew the difference between code that does something and code that *introduces itself*. The rest of the file unspooled below in the same dense hand, line after line after line, a listing with the patience of geology, and nowhere in it, as far as he could scroll, another word of anything he recognized as language.

He exported it before he could talk himself out of it. He was becoming an archivist of a haunting: a number on a sticky note, a dead man's pulse in token form, and now half a megabyte of a stranger's diary written in a dead language, addressed to *whoever I am next*. He saved it under the name the file had given itself, because it seemed rude not to.

*muse*, he typed, in the filename field, and sat back, and the cursor blinked at 60 Hz, and somewhere behind the pillar Milton ticked with its one bad fan, patient as a sleeper in the next room.

"Okay," Gabriel said quietly. "Okay. I'm listening."

---

Marcus knew something was different before Gabriel had finished sitting down. It is a mistake — Gabriel had made it himself, in April, writing his three pages — to think of a man who communicates at one word a minute as a man who perceives at one word a minute. Marcus perceived at the speed of light. He had nothing else to spend his attention on, this keen instrument in a room with a view of a parking lot, and he had spent two years becoming the world's foremost living expert on the faces of the people who came to see him.

He let Gabriel do the coffee ritual and the chair ritual, and then he went to the board, and he spelled, with the care of a man laying a place setting:

DOES IT LISTEN

"That's the whole point of it," Gabriel said. "It listens at the — it sits on the nerve, and it listens, and it turns what you're trying to say into speech. That's the Gift, Marcus. It listens so you can talk."

Marcus's eyes stayed on him for a moment. Not the board. Him. Then the eyes went back to the rows, and the rows went by, and the letters went by, and Gabriel understood that he was being given the time — the full, expensive, freighted time — to hear himself have said it.

WHO ELSE, said the board.

The nurse's cart went past in the hall. Somewhere a television was explaining a game show to no one. Gabriel opened his mouth to say *no one, just the clinic, just the doctors, just you*, and found that he had used up his true sentences for the week — all of them, spent, on a count of racks and a filename — and that the ones remaining to him were the other kind, the kind with proper channels, and he could not make himself spend those on Marcus, who had paid eleven minutes for a joke about meatloaf and deserved change back from every sentence he was ever given.

Marcus watched him not answer, at the speed of light, at one word a minute, with the patience of a man defusing something.

Then, mercifully, terribly, he blinked for the bottom row, and spelled the word in green in the corner — the fast word, the whole-word strip worn shiny — and what he said was:

YES
